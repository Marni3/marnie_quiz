Discrete mathematics is the study of 
mathematical structures that are fundamentally 
discrete rather than continuous. 
 
In contrast to real numbers that have the property 
of varying "smoothly", the objects studied in 
discrete mathematics do not vary smoothly in this 
way, but have distinct, separated values. 
 
Discrete mathematics therefore excludes topics in 
"continuous mathematics" such as calculus and 
analysis. 
 
The study of how discrete objects combine with 
one another and the probabilities of various 
outcomes is known as combinatorics.

Set Theory is the mathematical theory of well-
determined collections called sets. A set is an 
unordered collection of objects. The term object

is. The ‘objects’ that belongs to a given set is 
called an element or members of that set.

Order  
Order in sets does not mean the order of the 
elements but the size of the set. In sets, it doesn’t 
matter what order the elements are in. Another 
term for order is cardinality. 
 
{1, 2, 3, 4} is the same set as {3, 2, 4, 1} 
Superset  
A set A is a superset of another set B if all 
elements of the set B are elements of the set A. 
The superset relationship is denoted as A⊃B. 
 
Example: 
A {cat, dog, hen, bird} and B {cat, dog, hen}, 
then A⊃B but B ⊃ A. Since A contains elements 
not in B, the A is a proper superset of B. 
 
Disjoint Sets  
Two sets A and B are called disjoint if A and B 
have no elements in common.  Another 
equivalent definition of disjoint sets would be 
that the intersection of the two sets actually 
equals the empty set. 
 
Examples of disjoint sets: 
1. {1, 2, 3} and {a, b, c} 
2. {Carlo, Enzo, Judd} and {Kaye, Lynn, Honey} 
Venn Diagram 
A Venn diagram uses overlapping 
circles or other shapes to illustrate 
the logical relationships between two 
or more sets of items. Venn diagrams 
were conceived around 1880 by John 
Venn.
